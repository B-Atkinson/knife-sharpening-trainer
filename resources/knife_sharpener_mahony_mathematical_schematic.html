Knife Sharpener IMU
Mathematical Schematic

Bench calibration + 6-DOF Mahony attitude estimation + geometric sharpening-angle measurement
Implementation draft v0.3  •  Quaternion convention [w, x, y, z]  •  Filter angular rates in rad/s  •  No Euler-angle extraction

Purpose. This document is intended to be precise enough to translate directly into Rust. The Mahony filter estimates a full 3D unit quaternion. The user-facing sharpening angle is then computed geometrically from plane normals rather than from Euler pitch/roll/yaw.

Core design decision. The attitude estimator and the sharpening-angle measurement are separate concerns. Mahony maintains the best estimate of orientation; the application computes one scalar angle from that orientation using vector geometry.

1. Non-negotiable assumptions and coordinate conventions

X/Y/Z
Ideal physical mounting: \(+X_I\) runs along the blade toward the handle; \(+Y_I\) runs across the blade from cutting edge toward spine; \(+Z_I = +X_I \times +Y_I\) is normal to the IMU board / blade face. The geometric output calculation does not require perfect X/Y alignment.

Define two frames:

  • \(I\): current IMU/body frame. This frame moves with the sensor.
  • \(C\): calibration/reference frame. This frame is frozen when bench calibration completes.

The primary attitude state is the unit quaternion

\[ {}^{C}_{I}\hat q = \begin{bmatrix}q_w & q_x & q_y & q_z\end{bmatrix}^{T}, \qquad \left\|{}^{C}_{I}\hat q\right\|=1. \]

Its meaning is:

Frame meaning. \({}^{C}_{I}\hat q\) rotates a vector expressed in the current body frame \(I\) into the fixed calibration frame \(C\).

Quaternion component order is always

\[ \boxed{[w,x,y,z]} \]

and never \([x,y,z,w]\). Gyroscope values are converted to radians/second before they enter filter math. Internal angles are radians; degrees are only a UI/logging representation.

Calibration assumption. During bench-rest calibration, the blade face must be flat on a level reference surface whose plane is the desired \(0^\circ\) sharpening plane. This is what lets the accelerometer measurement identify the blade-plane normal without requiring exact knowledge of IMU-to-blade mounting alignment.

2. Mathematical primitives used everywhere

Implement these as small, separately tested functions before implementing the filter.

2.1 Vector norm and normalization

For a 3-vector \(\mathbf u=[u_x,u_y,u_z]^T\): \[ \|\mathbf u\|=\sqrt{u_x^2+u_y^2+u_z^2} \] \[ \operatorname{normalize}(\mathbf u)=\frac{\mathbf u}{\|\mathbf u\|}. \]

Never normalize a vector whose norm is near zero. Treat that sample as invalid instead.

2.2 Dot product

\[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=u_xv_x+u_yv_y+u_zv_z. \]

The result is a scalar. For unit vectors, it is the cosine of their separation angle.

2.3 3D cross product

\[ \mathbf u\times\mathbf v= \begin{bmatrix} u_yv_z-u_zv_y\\ u_zv_x-u_xv_z\\ u_xv_y-u_yv_x \end{bmatrix}. \]

The result is a 3D vector perpendicular to both inputs. This is the operation used to form the Mahony accelerometer innovation.

2.4 Quaternion conjugate and inverse

For \[ q=\begin{bmatrix}q_w&q_x&q_y&q_z\end{bmatrix}^T, \] its conjugate is \[ q^*=\operatorname{conj}(q)= \begin{bmatrix}q_w&-q_x&-q_y&-q_z\end{bmatrix}^T. \] For a unit quaternion, \[ q^{-1}=q^*. \]

We normalize the attitude quaternion after every numerical integration step so this remains true.

2.5 Hamilton quaternion multiplication — not a cross product

For \(p=[p_w,p_x,p_y,p_z]^T\) and \(q=[q_w,q_x,q_y,q_z]^T\):

\[ p\otimes q= \begin{bmatrix} p_wq_w-p_xq_x-p_yq_y-p_zq_z\\ p_wq_x+p_xq_w+p_yq_z-p_zq_y\\ p_wq_y-p_xq_z+p_yq_w+p_zq_x\\ p_wq_z+p_xq_y-p_yq_x+p_zq_w \end{bmatrix}. \]
Operator rule. \(\otimes\) always means Hamilton quaternion multiplication in this document. \(\times\) always means a 3D vector cross product.

2.6 Pure quaternions and vector_part()

A 3D vector \(\mathbf u=[u_x,u_y,u_z]^T\) can be embedded in quaternion algebra as the pure quaternion

\[ U=\begin{bmatrix}0&u_x&u_y&u_z\end{bmatrix}^T. \]

Define

\[ \operatorname{vector\_part} \left( \begin{bmatrix}w&x&y&z\end{bmatrix}^T \right) = \begin{bmatrix}x&y&z\end{bmatrix}^T. \]

So yes: vector_part() simply discards the quaternion's scalar component \(w\) and returns the remaining three components. When the quaternion represents a rotated pure vector, the scalar component should be zero mathematically and may only contain tiny floating-point residue numerically.

2.7 Rotate a 3D vector with \({}^{C}_{I}\hat q\)

To rotate \({}^{I}\mathbf u\) from the current IMU frame into the calibration frame, first form the pure quaternion \(U_I=[0,{}^{I}\mathbf u]^T\), then:

\[ U_C={}^{C}_{I}\hat q\otimes U_I\otimes\left({}^{C}_{I}\hat q\right)^*. \] Therefore \[ \boxed{ \operatorname{rotate}_{I\rightarrow C}(q,\mathbf u) = \operatorname{vector\_part} \left(q\otimes[0,\mathbf u]\otimes q^*\right) } \] The opposite transformation is \[ \boxed{ \operatorname{rotate}_{C\rightarrow I}(q,\mathbf u) = \operatorname{vector\_part} \left(q^*\otimes[0,\mathbf u]\otimes q\right). } \]

4. Phase 1 — Bench-rest calibration

Goal: establish a repeatable zero-angle reference, estimate startup gyro bias, identify the blade/reference-plane normal, and initialize the Mahony state.

4.1 Enter calibration only after stillness is detected

After the user requests calibration, wait until the device appears stationary, then start the five-second accumulation window. If stationarity is lost, discard that window and restart it.

For every candidate sample \(k\), compute \[ \omega_{mag,k}=\left\|{}^{I}\boldsymbol\omega_{m,k}\right\| \] and \[ a_{mag,k}=\left\|{}^{I}\mathbf a_{m,k}\right\|. \]

Exact stillness thresholds are empirical constants rather than part of Mahony itself. Log these values so they can be tuned from real hardware.

4.2 Average \(N\) stationary samples

At 200 Hz for five seconds, \(N\approx1000\). A running sum is sufficient; all samples do not need to be stored.

\[ {}^{I}\bar{\boldsymbol\omega} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{}^{I}\boldsymbol\omega_{m,k} \] \[ {}^{I}\bar{\mathbf a} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}{}^{I}\mathbf a_{m,k}. \]

4.3 Startup gyro bias

\[ \boxed{{}^{I}\mathbf b_0={}^{I}\bar{\boldsymbol\omega}} \]

Because the device is stationary during calibration, the true angular rate is approximately zero. Therefore the average measured gyro output is the startup bias estimate.

4.4 Calibration acceleration magnitude and direction

\[ a_0=\left\|{}^{I}\bar{\mathbf a}\right\| \] \[ {}^{C}\hat{\mathbf g}=\frac{{}^{I}\bar{\mathbf a}}{a_0}. \]

At calibration completion, \(C\equiv I_0\). Under the required flat-and-level calibration pose, the normalized acceleration direction is perpendicular to the blade/reference plane. Therefore store:

\[ {}^{I}\hat{\mathbf n}_{blade}={}^{C}\hat{\mathbf g} \] \[ {}^{C}\hat{\mathbf n}_0={}^{C}\hat{\mathbf g}. \]
Why this removes mounting-angle dependence. If the blade face is flat and level, the stationary accelerometer direction is perpendicular to the blade plane. The measured unit vector therefore tells us the blade-normal direction in the sensor's own axes even if the IMU package is not mounted perfectly square to the blade.

4.5 Initialize quaternion and residual-bias state

At calibration completion, by definition the current body frame and calibration frame are identical:

\[ \boxed{{}^{C}_{I}\hat q_0=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}^T} \] \[ {}^{I}\mathbf b_r=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\end{bmatrix}^T, \qquad w_{acc}=1. \]

5. Phase 2 — Raise blade and acquire the desired target angle

There is no separate attitude algorithm in this phase. Immediately after calibration, run the normal Mahony update loop in Section 6 at every IMU sample. The user raises the blade; the quaternion tracks the full 3D motion; the sharpening angle is computed geometrically after each update.

5.1 Current blade normal in the fixed calibration frame

\({}^{I}\hat{\mathbf n}_{blade}\) is a constant body-fixed unit vector identified during calibration. Rotate it with the current attitude estimate:

\[ {}^{C}\hat{\mathbf n} = \operatorname{normalize} \left( \operatorname{rotate}_{I\rightarrow C} \left({}^{C}_{I}\hat q,{}^{I}\hat{\mathbf n}_{blade}\right) \right). \]

5.2 Geometric sharpening angle — no Euler angles

The sharpening angle is the acute dihedral angle between the current blade plane and the calibrated reference plane. Plane normals make the calculation direct.

\[ c=\operatorname{clamp}\left({}^{C}\hat{\mathbf n}_0\cdot{}^{C}\hat{\mathbf n},-1,+1\right) \] \[ s=\left\|{}^{C}\hat{\mathbf n}_0\times{}^{C}\hat{\mathbf n}\right\| \] \[ \boxed{\theta_s=\operatorname{atan2}\left(s,|c|\right)}. \]

\(\theta_s\in[0,\pi/2]\). Convert to degrees only for display:

\[ \theta_{s,deg}=\theta_s\frac{180}{\pi}. \]
Why \(|c|\) is used. A plane has no intrinsic “front” normal. \(\mathbf n\) and \(-\mathbf n\) describe the same plane. The absolute dot product returns the acute plane-to-plane angle and remains valid if the knife is flipped to sharpen the opposite side.

5.3 Why this is cleaner than Euler pitch/roll

  • No rotation order is chosen, so there is no Euler-order coupling.
  • No gimbal singularity is introduced by the output calculation.
  • Intentional handle/belly-following motion may be a compound 3D rotation. If the blade plane remains at the same angle to the stone, \(\theta_s\) remains constant regardless of the normal's azimuth.
  • The 6-DOF system's unobservable yaw about gravity does not directly harm \(\theta_s\), because rotating the current plane normal around the reference normal preserves their mutual angle.

5.4 Set the target

Support either workflow:

  • Manual target: phone sends an angle, e.g. \(\theta_{target}=15^\circ=15\pi/180\ \mathrm{rad}\).
  • Capture target: user raises the knife to the desired displayed angle and presses Set Target; firmware stores \(\theta_{target}=\theta_s\).

Do not reset the quaternion when setting the target. Bench-rest calibration remains the zero reference for the entire run.

6. Phase 3 — Mahony maintenance/update loop

This same loop actually runs throughout Phases 2 and 3. Phase 3 simply adds comparison to \(\theta_{target}\) and audio/UI logic.

6.1 Read one time-aligned IMU sample

  • \({}^{I}\mathbf a_m\): measured accelerometer vector, m/s², current body frame.
  • \({}^{I}\boldsymbol\omega_m\): measured gyro vector, converted to rad/s, current body frame.
  • \(\Delta t\): measured elapsed time since prior update, seconds.

6.2 Validate and normalize accelerometer

\[ a_{mag}=\left\|{}^{I}\mathbf a_m\right\| \] \[ {}^{I}\hat{\mathbf a}=\frac{{}^{I}\mathbf a_m}{a_{mag}}. \]

If \(a_{mag}\) is below a small safety epsilon, skip accelerometer correction for this update. Do not normalize a near-zero vector.

6.3 Predict the fixed calibration reference in the current IMU frame

\({}^{C}\hat{\mathbf g}\) is fixed from calibration. Since \({}^{C}_{I}\hat q\) maps \(I\rightarrow C\), its conjugate maps \(C\rightarrow I\):

\[ {}^{I}\hat{\mathbf v} = \operatorname{normalize} \left( \operatorname{rotate}_{C\rightarrow I} \left({}^{C}_{I}\hat q,{}^{C}\hat{\mathbf g}\right) \right) \] or explicitly, \[ {}^{I}\hat{\mathbf v} = \operatorname{normalize} \left[ \operatorname{vector\_part} \left( q^*\otimes[0,{}^{C}\hat{\mathbf g}]\otimes q \right) \right]. \]

Interpretation: this is where the current quaternion estimate says the calibration acceleration direction should appear in current sensor coordinates.

6.4 Compute adaptive accelerometer confidence

This confidence policy is a project-specific wrapper around Mahony. It reduces accelerometer influence when knife translation makes the measurement inconsistent with the predicted gravity/reference vector.

\[ {}^{I}\mathbf a_{expected}=a_0\,{}^{I}\hat{\mathbf v} \] \[ {}^{I}\mathbf r_{acc}={}^{I}\mathbf a_m-{}^{I}\mathbf a_{expected} \] \[ \boxed{\rho=\frac{\left\|{}^{I}\mathbf r_{acc}\right\|}{a_0}}. \]

\(\rho\) is dimensionless. Because it uses the full vector residual, it can detect lateral linear acceleration that barely changes total \(|\mathbf a|\).

Recommended initial candidate-weight schedule:

\[ \rho_{full}=0.05,\qquad \rho_{soft}=0.20,\qquad \rho_{hard}=0.35. \]

For \(\rho\le\rho_{full}\):

\[ w_{candidate}=1. \]

For \(\rho_{full}<\rho<\rho_{soft}\):

\[ x=\frac{\rho-\rho_{full}}{\rho_{soft}-\rho_{full}} \] \[ S(x)=3x^2-2x^3 \] \[ w_{candidate}=1-0.90S(x). \]

For \(\rho_{soft}\le\rho<\rho_{hard}\):

\[ w_{candidate}=0.10. \]

For \(\rho\ge\rho_{hard}\), an invalid accelerometer norm, or an obviously corrupt measurement:

\[ w_{candidate}=0. \]
Why keep a 0.10 floor in the soft-reject region? A full-vector residual also grows if the attitude estimate itself becomes wrong. Keeping a small correction path helps the filter recover instead of becoming permanently locked out. Only severe measurements use \(w=0\).

Fast rejection, slow recovery

If candidate confidence is lower than current confidence, reject immediately:

\[ w_{candidate}If confidence is recovering, use a first-order recovery:

\[ \alpha=\frac{\Delta t}{\tau_{recover}+\Delta t} \] \[ w_{acc}\leftarrow w_{acc}+\alpha\left(w_{candidate}-w_{acc}\right) \] with initial test value \[ \boxed{\tau_{recover}=0.15\ \mathrm{s}}. \]

6.5 Compute Mahony innovation

\[ \boxed{{}^{I}\mathbf e_{raw}={}^{I}\hat{\mathbf a}\times{}^{I}\hat{\mathbf v}} \]

This is a 3D vector cross product. Both inputs are normalized unit vectors expressed in the current body frame.

\[ {}^{I}\mathbf e_M=w_{acc}\,{}^{I}\mathbf e_{raw}. \]

This second operation is ordinary scalar-vector multiplication.

6.6 Update residual gyro-bias estimate

Only let the integral/bias estimator learn from high-confidence accelerometer data:

\[ \text{if }w_{acc}\ge0.80\text{ and }\rho\le0.05: \qquad {}^{I}\mathbf b_r \leftarrow {}^{I}\mathbf b_r-K_i{}^{I}\mathbf e_M\Delta t. \]

Otherwise \({}^{I}\mathbf b_r\) is unchanged.

Use anti-windup by clamping each residual-bias component to \(\pm b_{r,max}\). Initial test value:

\[ \boxed{b_{r,max}=0.05\ \mathrm{rad/s}\approx2.86^\circ/\mathrm{s}}. \]

6.7 Correct the gyro

\[ \boxed{ {}^{I}\boldsymbol\omega_c = {}^{I}\boldsymbol\omega_m -{}^{I}\mathbf b_0 -{}^{I}\mathbf b_r +K_p{}^{I}\mathbf e_M }. \]

Every term is a three-vector in the current sensor axes. The result remains in rad/s.

6.8 Convert corrected angular rate to quaternion derivative

Construct the pure angular-rate quaternion \[ \Omega_I= \begin{bmatrix} 0&\omega_{c,x}&\omega_{c,y}&\omega_{c,z} \end{bmatrix}^T. \] Then \[ \boxed{ \dot q=\frac12\left(q\otimes\Omega_I\right) }. \]

\(\otimes\) is Hamilton quaternion multiplication. \(\dot q\) is a rate of change of orientation, not an orientation itself.

6.9 Integrate and normalize quaternion

Using first-order Euler integration: \[ q_{temp}=q_t+\dot q\,\Delta t \] \[ \boxed{q_{t+1}=\frac{q_{temp}}{\|q_{temp}\|}}. \]

The addition and scalar multiplication here are component-wise operations on all four quaternion components.

6.10 Compute geometric sharpening angle

\[ {}^{C}\hat{\mathbf n} = \operatorname{normalize} \left( \operatorname{rotate}_{I\rightarrow C} (q,{}^{I}\hat{\mathbf n}_{blade}) \right) \] \[ c=\operatorname{clamp}\left({}^{C}\hat{\mathbf n}_0\cdot{}^{C}\hat{\mathbf n},-1,+1\right) \] \[ s=\left\|{}^{C}\hat{\mathbf n}_0\times{}^{C}\hat{\mathbf n}\right\| \] \[ \boxed{\theta_s=\operatorname{atan2}(s,|c|)}. \]

This scalar is the application output. It is not an Euler angle.

7. Application error and tone logic

\[ \boxed{e_\theta=\theta_s-\theta_{target}}. \]

Do not confuse \(e_\theta\) with the three-axis Mahony innovation \({}^{I}\mathbf e_M\).

if angle_error < -deadband_rad:
    tone = LOW
elif angle_error > deadband_rad:
    tone = HIGH
else:
    tone = SILENT

Deadband is a product/UX parameter and should be tuned after actual angle-noise characterization.

8. Complete execution-order pseudocode

// ---------- CALIBRATION ----------
wait_until_stationary();
collect_stationary_samples_for_5_seconds();

b0_I      = mean(gyro_samples_rad_s);
a_bar_I   = mean(accel_samples_m_s2);
a0        = norm(a_bar_I);
g_hat_C   = normalize(a_bar_I);
n_blade_I = g_hat_C;
n0_C      = g_hat_C;

q_CI      = Quat(1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0);
b_r_I     = Vec3(0.0, 0.0, 0.0);
w_acc     = 1.0;

// ---------- EVERY IMU SAMPLE ----------
a_m_I      = read_accel_m_s2();
omega_m_I  = read_gyro_rad_s();
dt         = measured_dt_seconds();

// A. normalized measured acceleration
a_mag       = norm(a_m_I);
a_hat_I     = normalize(a_m_I);       // only if valid

// B. predicted calibration gravity direction in current body frame
v_hat_I     = rotate_C_to_I(q_CI, g_hat_C);
v_hat_I     = normalize(v_hat_I);

// C. adaptive accelerometer trust
a_expected  = a0 * v_hat_I;
r_acc_I     = a_m_I - a_expected;
rho         = norm(r_acc_I) / a0;
w_candidate = accel_weight_from_rho(rho);
w_acc       = fast_reject_slow_recover(w_acc, w_candidate, dt);

// D. Mahony innovation (VECTOR CROSS PRODUCT)
e_raw_I     = cross(a_hat_I, v_hat_I);
e_M_I       = w_acc * e_raw_I;

// E. online residual gyro-bias estimate
if w_acc >= 0.80 && rho <= 0.05 {
    b_r_I -= Ki * e_M_I * dt;
    b_r_I  = component_clamp(b_r_I, -b_r_max, +b_r_max);
}

// F. corrected gyro
omega_c_I = omega_m_I - b0_I - b_r_I + Kp * e_M_I;

// G. quaternion propagation (HAMILTON PRODUCT)
Omega_I = Quat(0.0, omega_c_I.x, omega_c_I.y, omega_c_I.z);
q_dot   = 0.5 * hamilton(q_CI, Omega_I);
q_CI    = normalize(q_CI + q_dot * dt);

// H. geometric sharpening angle
n_C       = normalize(rotate_I_to_C(q_CI, n_blade_I));
dot_n     = clamp(dot(n0_C, n_C), -1.0, +1.0);
cross_n   = norm(cross(n0_C, n_C));
theta_s   = atan2(cross_n, abs(dot_n));

// I. application comparison
angle_error = theta_s - theta_target;
update_audio_feedback(angle_error);

9. Rust-oriented data model recommendation

struct Vec3 {
    x: f32,
    y: f32,
    z: f32,
}

struct Quat {
    w: f32,
    x: f32,
    y: f32,
    z: f32,
}

struct Calibration {
    gyro_bias_start: Vec3,      // b0_I, rad/s
    accel_mag_ref: f32,         // a0, m/s^2
    accel_ref_c: Vec3,          // g_hat_C, unit
    blade_normal_body: Vec3,    // n_blade_I, unit
    reference_normal_c: Vec3,   // n0_C, unit
}

struct MahonyState {
    q_c_i: Quat,                // unit quaternion, I -> C
    gyro_bias_residual: Vec3,   // b_r_I, rad/s
    accel_weight: f32,          // w_acc in [0,1]
}

struct FilterConfig {
    kp: f32,                    // initial 0.5
    ki: f32,                    // initial 0.0
    rho_full: f32,              // initial 0.05
    rho_soft_reject: f32,       // initial 0.20
    rho_hard_reject: f32,       // initial 0.35
    accel_recovery_tau_s: f32,  // initial 0.15
    bias_residual_max_rad_s: f32, // initial 0.05
}

11. Gain and weighting tuning plan

The values below are initial test values, not universal constants. Keep them in the implementation so you have a rational place to start, then tune from logged real motions.

Recommended tuning order. Validate calibration first, then tune \(K_p\) with \(K_i=0\), then tune accelerometer-confidence thresholds, then introduce only enough \(K_i\) to correct residual long-term drift, and finally tune recovery timing. Changing several families at once makes symptoms difficult to diagnose.

\(\rho_{hard}\)0.35 baselineSame. The accelerometer is being completely rejected during common, nonproblematic sharpening motion, leaving excessive gyro-only operation. Increase \(\rho_{hard}\).
\(\tau_{recover}\)0.15 sHow cautiously accelerometer confidence returns after a disturbance. After a reversal, trust returns so quickly that a second visible correction/kick occurs before the acceleration transient has actually settled. Increase \(\tau_{recover}\). Confidence drops immediately on bad data, then returns smoothly as motion settles, without a secondary kick and without a perceptible long delay.
\(\tau_{recover}\)0.15 s baselineSame. The blade has clearly returned to steady motion or rest but \(w_{acc}\) remains suppressed noticeably longer than necessary. Decrease \(\tau_{recover}\).
\(b_{r,max}\)0.05 rad/sSafety bound on learned residual gyro bias. Logs show the residual bias repeatedly hitting the clamp during valid long stationary runs, while independent gyro data confirms a larger real residual bias. Increase the bound cautiously. Normal learned residual bias stays comfortably inside the bound; the clamp acts only as a fault/anti-windup guard rather than a routine operating limit.
\(b_{r,max}\)0.05 rad/s baselineSame. A bad dynamic event can drive the learned bias to implausibly large values before the clamp catches it. Decrease the bound.

11.1 How to know when tuning is “good”

A good value is generally found at the boundary between two failure modes, not at an arbitrary number. For \(K_p\), for example:

\[ \text{too low: sluggish correction} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\text{useful operating region}} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{too high: noisy / acceleration-sensitive correction}. \]

The desired value is the one that meets your response requirement while leaving margin before the opposite failure mode appears. Similar logic applies to every threshold and gain.

11.2 Controlled tuning tests

Test A — Static repeatability. Place the blade at mechanically known angles multiple times.

Good: the reported angle returns to the same value with low spread and no meaningful drift.

If not: solve calibration/mechanical repeatability problems before tuning gains around them.

Test B — Step response. Move from 0° to a known angle, stop, and log the settling transient.

Too slow: increase \(K_p\).

Jittery/oversensitive: decrease \(K_p\).

Test C — Long stationary hold. Hold the blade still for several minutes after startup calibration.

Slow persistent drift: after confirming startup bias and \(K_p\), increase \(K_i\) slightly.

Persistent bias after earlier motion: decrease \(K_i\) or tighten the confidence gate.

Test D — Normal stroke. Perform realistic sharpening strokes while mechanically trying to preserve true angle.

Angle disturbance while confidence stays high: lower \(\rho_{full}\) and/or \(\rho_{soft}\).

Too much gyro-only time with otherwise clean data: raise the relevant threshold.

Test E — Stroke reversal. Reverse direction deliberately and inspect \(\rho\), \(w_{acc}\), and \(\theta_s\).

Spike before rejection: lower \(\rho_{hard}\) or \(\rho_{soft}\).

Second kick on recovery: increase \(\tau_{recover}\).

Recovery feels delayed after motion is clearly calm: decrease \(\tau_{recover}\).

Test F — Aggressive-motion rejection. Exaggerate linear accelerations while keeping the intended blade angle as constant as practical.

Good: confidence falls, the estimator propagates primarily from the gyro during the transient, and the sharpening-angle trace does not falsely follow the linear acceleration.

11.3 What to log during tuning

At minimum log: raw accel XYZ, raw gyro XYZ, startup bias, residual bias, \(\rho\), \(w_{candidate}\), \(w_{acc}\), raw Mahony innovation XYZ, weighted innovation XYZ, quaternion, \(\theta_s\), target angle, angle error, and \(\Delta t\). Without these signals, two different failure mechanisms can look identical in the final angle trace.

13. Important limitation of a 6-DOF IMU

Accelerometer + gyro alone cannot establish absolute heading about gravity. Mahony can stabilize gravity-referenced tilt, but yaw may drift. For this application that is largely benign: \(\theta_s\) depends on the angle between the current blade-plane normal and the calibrated reference-plane normal, and a pure rotation around the reference normal does not change that angle. If future features require absolute blade heading in the stone plane, an additional heading reference would be required.

14. Calibration-surface caveat and fallback

Important. The arbitrary-mounting benefit depends on the blade being flat on a level reference plane during calibration, so the stationary accelerometer direction is also the blade-plane normal.

If the final product must calibrate on a reference plane that may be significantly tilted relative to gravity, a single static 6-DOF measurement cannot simultaneously infer both gravity direction and an arbitrarily mounted blade-plane normal. In that case choose one of these designs:

  • enforce a known mechanical mounting relationship, such as sensor \(+Z\) accurately normal to the blade face, and use that known body vector as the blade normal; or
  • add another calibration pose/motion that identifies the blade frame relative to the IMU.

For the initial prototype, a level flat calibration pose is the simplest architecture.
